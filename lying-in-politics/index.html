The problem, in five steps

Lying in politics.

The Public Office (Accountability) Bill creates an offence of misleading the public. It is the most consequential provision in the Bill and the least discussed. This page is simply a record of what the offence is for.

Four films and the evidence behind them. It takes about fifteen minutes.

Prepared for Members of the House of Lords considering Amendment 66A. Every factual claim below is sourced, and the sources are linked.

Step one

The men responsible tell the truth far too late.

We begin outside British politics, and outside party politics, because the argument made on this page is not about a party and not about a country. It is about what happens when people who hold public office deceive the public who pay for it.

Robert McNamara was the United States Secretary of Defense through the escalation of the Vietnam War. Decades afterwards he set out, on camera, what he had come to believe about the decisions he took and the way they were explained to the public at the time.

Film oneA Lesson in History

Every man on this page admitted something, or was found out. All of it came years after the decisions were taken, and years after the damage was done.

That is the pattern this page is about. By the time the truth is established, the war has been fought, the vote has been held, the money has been spent and the government has changed. There is no remedy left, because nothing was ever tested while it mattered.

Step two

A country taken to war.

In 2003 the United Kingdom invaded Iraq. The case put to the public rested, in part, on intelligence about weapons that could be deployed at short notice.

Film twoIraq, 2003

  • The claim at the centre of the September 2002 dossier, that Iraq could deploy chemical or biological weapons within 45 minutes, rested on a single uncorroborated source, reported to have come from an Iraqi who had overheard a conversation in the back of a taxi two years earlier.
  • Intelligence staff objected to its inclusion. Most of the dossier was double sourced. This was not, and they believed the source was wrong.
  • The Iraq Inquiry under Sir John Chilcot found that the agencies had supplied flawed intelligence, that the reports were not challenged when they should have been, and that the certainty expressed in public was not backed by the evidence held at the time.
  • In February 2003 Downing Street issued a briefing document on Iraqi concealment, presented to journalists as intelligence material. Whole sections had been copied, word for word and typing mistake for typing mistake, from the published work of a postgraduate student, Ibrahim al-Marashi, drawn from a thesis chapter about events twelve years earlier. It credited nobody.
  • The copied text was not reproduced as found. It was hardened. “Monitoring” foreign embassies became “spying” on them. “Aiding opposition groups” became “supporting terrorist organisations”.
  • In November 2003 the public were told that the remains of 400,000 people had been found in mass graves in Iraq. In July 2004 The Observer reported that the number of bodies actually recovered at that point was under five thousand. The figure had never been a count of anything.

Sources: the Chilcot report, as reported by NBC News · Ibrahim al-Marashi on his plagiarised work · The Observer, 18 July 2004

Nobody has ever been prosecuted in this country for misleading the public about any of it, because until this Bill there was no offence capable of being used. There still is not one that reaches a Member of Parliament.

Step three

A promise made to a generation, in writing.

Before the 2010 general election, Liberal Democrat candidates signed a National Union of Students pledge to vote against any increase in tuition fees. They were photographed holding it. In government, the cap was raised to nine thousand pounds.

Film threeNo more broken promises

In September 2012 the party leader issued a public apology. He apologised for making the pledge, saying it had been a mistake to promise something the party could not deliver. The National Union of Students answered that he should have apologised for breaking it.

That distinction is the whole of the argument. An apology for having made a promise is not accountability. It is a press release.

No law was broken, because there is no law. The only sanction available was electoral, and it arrived years later, applied to a whole party rather than to any decision, and settled nothing about what anybody knew or intended at the time they said it.

Sources: Times Higher Education, on the apology and the NUS response

Step four

A referendum, and a number that kept changing.

In 2016 the United Kingdom voted on its membership of the European Union. A figure for what that membership cost was placed on the side of a bus, and repeated for months.

Film fourA United Kingdom divided by deception

The point is not that one number was wrong. It is that the numbers never agreed with each other, and every one of them described the same thing.

  • In a Telegraph column on 16 March 2016: leaving would “save £8 billion a year”.
  • In The Sun: “the £8.5 billion a year we sent to Brussels and never see again”.
  • On ITV’s Late Debate: “net, 10 billion pounds, between 8 and 10 billion pounds”.
  • At Mill Hill Broadway: “£20 billion pounds every year to the EU, £350 million a week”.
  • At High Wycombe, in front of the bus: “huge sums of money, a £10 billion a week”.

Sources: The Telegraph column, 16 March 2016 · the column in The Sun

Film fiveThe numbers, side by side

Two further facts matter, because they go to capacity rather than to truth.

  • On 4 March 2016 the Mayor of London’s Chief of Staff wrote to the Mayor’s senior staff that “as with other mayoral policies” the position on leaving the EU would receive support from Greater London Authority officers, and that he expected them “either to advocate the Mayor’s position or otherwise not openly to contradict it”. The Mayor’s spokesman confirmed it publicly. This was not a private citizen expressing a view. It was the office of the Mayor of London adopting a position and instructing publicly funded staff to support it.
  • Vote Leave’s campaign director was asked, in his own written account seven months later, whether the campaign would have won without the £350 million claim and the message built on it. He answered: “All our research and the close result strongly suggests No.” He called it “clearly the most effective argument”.

Sources: BBC News, 8 March 2016 · ITV News London, 8 March 2016 · Dominic Cummings, On the referendum #21, January 2017

A private prosecution was brought. It never reached a jury. The proceedings were consumed by the question of what capacity the defendant had been speaking in, and the court never decided whether anything said to the public had been true or false.

Step five

What it has cost, measured.

None of the above is contested history for the public. They watched all of it. The result is not cynicism about one party. It is a settled belief about the entire profession, and it is measurable.

The evidence

Polling, research and the legislative record.

Trust

Ipsos has run its Veracity Index since 1983, asking the British public which professions they trust to tell the truth. The 2025 edition is the twenty-eighth, conducted with 1,022 British adults aged 16 and over between 5 and 11 November 2025.

9%trust politicians generally to tell the truth. Joint lowest in the forty-two years the index has run.
14%trust government ministers to tell the truth.
1stNurses are the most trusted profession in Britain, as they have been for years. They are covered by this Bill. Politicians are not.

Ipsos Veracity Index 2025

What the public want done about it

Polling by Opinium, commissioned by the campaign group Compassion in Politics, found 73 per cent support for making deliberate lying by politicians a criminal offence, including 71 per cent of Conservative voters and 79 per cent of Labour voters. It is campaign-commissioned research and should be read as such, but no poll we are aware of has ever found majority opposition.

We say this plainly because the opposite is sometimes assumed in debate: there is no public constituency for exempting legislators. The disagreement is in this building, not outside it.

What other legislatures have done

In 2024 the Senedd considered a clause that would have made deliberate deception an offence and disqualified any member or candidate convicted of it for four years. The clause was removed before the Bill became law, by 26 votes to 13. The Welsh Government undertook to bring forward an alternative route to disqualification through an independent judicial process.

We include this because it is the most instructive precedent available, and it does not flatter our case. It is the second time in three years that a legislature in these islands has been asked to apply a deception rule to itself and has declined.

Which of these would the Bill, as drafted, have reached?

A Prime Minister, speaking as Prime Minister, would be covered. So would every civil servant, every police officer, every nurse and every head teacher in the country.

No Member of the House of Commons. No Member of the House of Lords. No Member of the Scottish Parliament, Senedd Cymru or the Northern Ireland Assembly. And, because the Bill never defines what acting “in their capacity as such” means, a great deal of court time would be spent arguing about which hat somebody was wearing before anybody asked whether the public had been deceived.

Amendment 66A to the Public Office (Accountability) Bill fixes both. It adds every legislator in the United Kingdom, it protects parliamentary privilege and honestly held political opinion on its face, and it defines capacity, functions and harm.
