Why we expect to win

The Hillsborough Law has to be a success. Without lawmakers, it won’t be.

The Prime Minister needs the Hillsborough Law to be a huge success. A law that makes lying to the public a crime for everyone except the people who make the law would be attacked by the press and the public as hypocritical. The damage to trust in the UK’s parliaments would be disastrous, and it would spoil what should be a proud achievement.

Support for including lawmakers is plain to see, from peers and MPs to people in the street. That is why we think it is very likely the Bill will be amended to include them.

Every signature makes that harder to refuse.

What our amendment covers

Parliament stays protected. Public statements don’t.

Our amendment does not apply the offence of misleading the public, the duty of candour or any other part of the Hillsborough Law to anything MPs, peers or members of any of the UK’s parliaments say or do in the proceedings of those parliaments. We accept that those proceedings must stay protected.

It asks only that lawmakers answer to the same law as everyone else when they speak to the public outside Parliament.

Not coveredAnything said or done in the proceedings of any UK parliament
CoveredWhat they tell the public outside: on television, on the radio, online and at public events

      The privilege excuse

      Privilege was never the reason

      The government’s excuse for leaving MPs and peers out is parliamentary privilege. The minister, Lord Lemos, told the House of Lords on 12 October:

      “There is a long-standing principle of parliamentary privilege and I do not think the Bill is the right place for that.”

      But our amendment doesn’t touch what MPs and peers say in Parliament. Conservative peer Lord Wolfson told the House:

      “It is true that Amendment 66A excludes what is said in Parliament.”

      Nine days later, the government’s own Deputy Chief Whip, Baroness Anderson, told the Lords the courts would not read the new offences as setting aside the protections for “what Members say or do in Parliament.”

      How these figures are made

      Only people who signed and then confirmed by email are counted. Every figure is a total: we never publish anyone's email, postcode or individual answers, and any group of fewer than 10 people is shown as "fewer than 10".

      Constituencies are worked out inside our own systems from the first part of each signer's postcode (for example "NR2 2"), using the Office for National Statistics' lookup of every UK postcode to the July 2024 constituencies. Where that area crosses a boundary, its signers are shared between the constituencies in proportion. No postcode is sent to anyone else. Signers who live abroad are counted in the totals but not on the map.

      Each statement is shown with the dates it was asked. The stream at the top shows the first name and town of people who signed today. Each count opens on the total at midnight and adds today’s signers one by one until it reaches today’s total, then rises only when someone new signs. The days since launch are counted from 26 October, when we first emailed our backers about the petition. Every one of the 650 constituencies has at least one signer whose postcode area lies wholly or mostly inside it. Emails to MPs are our estimate: signers who copied their MP’s address, the subject and the letter (before 9 November, signers who copied the letter). We cannot see whether they pressed send. Once today has 100 signers, the stream goes round today’s signers again; the numbers only rise for new signatures. Hexagon map layout by Open Innovations (CC BY-SA 4.0).
